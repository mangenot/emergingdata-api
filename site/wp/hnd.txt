[ed_overview date="21 November 2026"]
[ed_standfirst]Honduras ends 2026 with the soundest accounts of the five countries covered and the weakest order book. Reserves of USD 11.6 billion, a bond of USD 816 million placed at 6.4% to retire the January 2027 maturity, a budget deficit held at 1% of GDP and the last review of the IMF programme agreed on 1 November stand against maquila exports down 9.6% in eight months, 50,000 jobs lost since 2022 and an industry that says Nicaragua is 60% cheaper. Inflation reached 6.98% in October on fuel a third dearer than a year ago, the central bank raised its rate to 6% in September, and the maquila wage for 2027 is negotiated in December with the unions asking 9 to 11%.[/ed_standfirst]
<p>On 19 November the government declared a partial state of exception in three municipalities of Yoro, Yorito, Sulaco and Victoria, a day after twelve people were killed in four shootings claimed by a local cartel; the measure, with a night curfew, may be extended to neighbouring parts of Francisco Morazán and Comayagua. It is the first since Nasry Asfura lifted the national state of exception of December 2022 on taking office in January, and Yoro borders the Sula valley where the maquila is.</p>
<p>The week brought two prices and a demand. On 12 November the central bank reported inflation of 6.98% for October, the highest since 2022, with fuel 46% dearer than a year earlier and the drought in the food basket; on 16 November petrol fell by almost five lempiras a gallon while diesel stayed at L 153.53, where a subsidy of L 5.05 a gallon has held it since late September. On 18 November the maquila unions asked the Labour ministry to convene the tripartite commission, so that the 2027 wage is agreed in December and paid from January.</p>
<p>On 1 November the IMF mission and the government reached staff agreement on the sixth and final review of the 2023 programme: USD 120 million to be released by the board in late December, growth of 3.4% expected for 2026 after 3.7% in the first half, a current account surplus of 4.1% of GDP in the first half turning into a deficit of 2.2% for the year, and a deficit target of 1% of GDP for 2026 and 2027. Nothing has been said of a successor.</p>
<h3>The latest data</h3>
<p>Inflation rose from 3.46% in February to 5.56% in April, when the fuel prices of the war in the Gulf arrived, eased to 5.58% in July and climbed again to 6.20% in August, 6.28% in September and 6.98% in October, against a target band of 3 to 5%. Transport contributes 3.2 points and food 1.8; the fuel price index is 46.2% above a year earlier, the largest rise in the series, and the bank estimates that without the subsidies and the rate rise inflation would be 7.83%. Core inflation is 3.43%. The policy rate went from 5.75% to 6% on 18 September, the first move since October 2024; the bank calls its stance neutral and expects the transmission to lending rates to take six to nine months.</p>
<p>The external accounts are the strong part. Reserves were USD 11.6 billion on 22 October, 6.7 months of imports, after a record 11.85 billion in July; remittances were USD 11.23 billion in January to October, 10.8% more than a year earlier, and 56 of every 100 dollars that enter the country. Private credit grows 8.8% a year, 13.8% to firms. Growth was 3.8% in 2025, 3.7% in the first half of 2026 and is expected at 3.4% for the year by the IMF, inside the central bank's 3 to 4% range; unemployment rose to 7.1% in the 2026 household survey from 4.9%.</p>
<h3>The fuel bill</h3>
<p>Honduras imports all of its fuel and buys most of its thermal power on contracts indexed to it. Diesel in Tegucigalpa went from L 84.36 a gallon in January to L 153.53 at the end of September, 82% more, and has not moved since because the state pays L 5.05 of every gallon; regular petrol peaked at L 140.97 in October and was cut to L 128.05 on 16 November as the world price eased. The government put the cost of the fuel support at about USD 49 million in September; the IMF asks that the subsidies stay temporary and targeted.</p>
<p>Electricity followed with a lag. The regulator raised ENEE tariffs by 4.11% in January, 10.49% in April and 12.48% in July, about 29% in nine months, and approved a further 0.64% for the fourth quarter that the state absorbs: the Ley de Energía Eléctrica sanctioned on 29 September freezes tariffs for six months from its publication, with the deferred amounts carried forward and liable to interest. The medium-voltage rate stands at L 4.33 per kWh, about 16 US cents, and the high-voltage rate at 15 cents, the dearest grid of the five countries covered, on a network that loses 31.5% of its output.</p>
<h3>The maquila's year</h3>
<p>Exports of goods for transformation, the maquila, were USD 3.29 billion in January to August, 9.6% less than a year earlier, and the Honduran Maquiladora Association expects the year to close 10% down; textiles account for three quarters of the fall and wire harnesses for most of the rest. US imports of apparel from Honduras were USD 1.9 billion over the twelve months to August, 10% below the previous twelve, and USD 2.0 billion in 2025 against USD 3.2 billion in 2022, the peak. Employment has gone from more than 170,000 to about 130,000, and the association counts 50,000 direct jobs lost since 2022.</p>
<p>The closures of the year tell where the orders went. Gildan cut 1,000 jobs at its Río Nance sock plant in Choloma in January and closed it in June, moving the production to El Salvador; Fruit of the Loom closed its Merendón plant, also in Choloma, in December 2025 with 1,000 workers. The association puts Nicaragua 60% and El Salvador 25% below Honduras in cost, and names wages, electricity and the investment climate in that order. The duty position is unchanged and good: CAFTA-DR apparel enters the United States at zero, exempted from the Section 301 duties of 24 July, and the agreement has no expiry.</p>
<h3>The wage round</h3>
<p>The maquila has its own scale, negotiated between the association and the unions in multi-year agreements: HNL 11,137 in 2024, 11,972 in 2025 and 12,930 in 2026, rises of 6.5%, 7.5% and 8%, under an agreement that ends in December. At 26.9 lempiras to the dollar the 2026 wage is USD 481 a month, the highest of the five countries covered and more than three times Kenya's. The unions have not published a figure; the sector expects 9 to 11%, and the general minimum wage agreed in April gives the reference: 6%, 7% and 7.5% for 2027 by size of firm, with a clause that lifts the rise to the December inflation rate if that is higher.</p>
<p>The arithmetic for a buyer is in dollars. The lempira has depreciated 2.6% over the twelve months to October and inflation runs at 7%, so the real rate is close to flat, about 1% dearer, and the dollar wage moves with the agreement, not with the currency: the 8% of 2026 became 4.8% in dollars, a 10% rise in January would be about 7% in dollars. The association's case is that this is the gap with Nicaragua and El Salvador; the unions' case is the cost of the basic basket at 7% inflation.</p>
<h3>The new government</h3>
<p>Nasry Asfura took office on 27 January 2026 after the contested count of November 2025, with the Partido Nacional holding 49 of 128 seats; the energy reform of 1 September passed with 78 votes, 49 of his own and 29 from the Liberals and others, and the 2027 budget of L 455 billion, about USD 17 billion and 2.5% more than 2026, is due for a vote between 14 and 19 December. The relationship with Washington is the axis of the government: a meeting with the US president at Mar-a-Lago on 7 February opened working groups on tariffs, and Honduras keeps the 2023 recognition of Beijing under review without having decided.</p>
<p>Security was the campaign and is the risk. The national state of exception was lifted in January; homicides were 2,120 from 1 January to 27 October, 0.8% fewer than a year earlier, a rate of 20.8 per 100,000 against 21.3; August was the worst month of the year, and the massacre of 18 November in Yoro brought the first partial state of exception. The maquila's own exposure is extortion on transport and in the industrial suburbs of San Pedro Sula, which the national figures do not measure.</p>
<h3>The financial position</h3>
<p>The 36-month programme signed with the IMF in September 2023 ends with the sixth review; its disbursements, the Eurobond of July and the remittances have taken reserves from USD 6.7 billion in October 2024 to 11.6 billion. The USD 700 million bond of 2017 matures in January 2027 and is covered by the USD 816 million ten-year issue of 22 July at 6.40%, two points below the 8.625% of 2024, with USD 115 million of new money. Public debt is about 40% of GDP, the deficit target 1%, debt service 16% of the 2027 budget; S&P holds BB- with a stable outlook since March, Moody's B1 since July, the best ratings of the five.</p>
<p>The central bank is changing the way dollars are sold. Since April 2023 it has allocated them through a daily auction; from early 2027 it will restore the interbank market over eighteen to twenty-four months, with banks keeping 10% of the dollars they buy at first and the bank still serving fuel importers and power generators directly. The lempira slid 1.7% in the first eight months against 3% in 2024 and 4% in 2025, and the governor expects 27 to the dollar at the end of the year; the IMF describes the move as the groundwork for inflation targeting.</p>
<h3>What it means for buyers</h3>
<p>The cost base for 2027 is a wage decision in December, a tariff that cannot rise before April and then catches up, diesel at a world price the state partly pays, and a currency that moves 2 to 3% a year by design. None of it is a shock; all of it is dearer than the neighbours, which is why the order book is the risk and the macro data are not. The duty advantage over Asia, zero against 10 to 27%, is intact and unexpiring, and the port is two days from the US Gulf.</p>
<p>Three checks at supplier level: the wage agreement when it is signed, with the allowances that sit on top of the scale; the power bill after April 2027, when the deferred increases return with interest; and the security situation on the roads from the Sula valley to Puerto Cortés, where the partial state of exception of November is the first sign of a return of the measure.</p>

[ed_lead]The best logistics score of the five countries covered and the shortest route to the US market: nine tenths of exports leave through Puerto Cortés, two days from the US Gulf, with about 900,000 containers expected in 2026. The risks of the year were weather and fuel, not capacity: an El Niño season with few storms but floods in San Pedro Sula in October, diesel 82% dearer since January and a transport strike in September. Drewry's index was USD 4,305 per 40ft on 12 November; the route itself is not priced.[/ed_lead]
[ed_text]
The maquila is concentrated in the Sula valley around San Pedro Sula and Choloma, an hour from Puerto Cortés, the main Central American port on the Caribbean and the gateway for the US East Coast and Gulf; about 90% of Honduran exports leave through it, container traffic was 0.9 million TEU in 2024 (World Bank) and the logistics council expects about 900,000 containers in 2026.
The Logistics Performance Index of 2023 gives Honduras 2.9 out of 5 and 2.8 on customs, the best of the five countries covered; the liner connectivity index of 11.8 is a tenth of Pakistan's, because the port serves the Americas and not the mainline Asia trades.
The 2026 hurricane season has been quiet, as El Niño forecasts said it would be, with fewer than seven cyclones expected in the Atlantic and none landing in Honduras; the rains of 3 to 5 October still flooded sectors of San Pedro Sula and put the Sula valley under Copeco alerts, and 103 of the 298 municipalities were on red alert for drought in September.
Fuel is the logistics cost that moved: diesel in Tegucigalpa went from L 84.36 a gallon in January to L 153.53 at the end of September and has been held there by a subsidy of L 5.05 a gallon; transport operators blocked roads in Tegucigalpa and San Pedro Sula on 28 September.
On the Asia lanes the Drewry World Container Index stood at USD 4,305 per 40ft on 12 November, down 2% on the week, with Shanghai to New York at USD 9,020; no public index quotes Puerto Cortés, and the chapter carries the Drewry lanes as the trend until one does.

[ed_section key="ENE"]
[ed_lead]The dearest grid of the five countries covered and the one that moved most this year: ENEE tariffs rose 4.11%, 10.49% and 12.48% in the first three quarters, about 29%, before a law froze them for six months from October and the state took the fourth-quarter rise. Medium-voltage power costs L 4.33 per kWh, about 16 US cents, on a network losing 31.5% of its output; diesel is 82% dearer than in January; the deferred increases return, with interest, in the spring.[/ed_lead]
[ed_text]
The regulator adjusts ENEE tariffs each quarter with fuel costs and the exchange rate: +4.11% in January, +10.49% in April and +12.48% in July 2026, as the war in the Gulf went through the thermal contracts, and +0.64% approved for October that is not charged.
The Ley de Energía Eléctrica, approved on 1 September with 78 votes and sanctioned on 29 September, freezes ENEE tariffs for six months from its publication, the state absorbing the difference and the deferred amounts carried forward and liable to interest; it also splits ENEE into generation, transmission and distribution subsidiaries, ends direct contracting in favour of tenders and reverse auctions, and keeps the hydro plants in state hands, with 96 votes needed for any sale.
The fourth-quarter schedule puts medium voltage at L 4.3305 per kWh, about 16.1 US cents at 26.9 lempiras, high voltage at L 4.0917, 15.2 cents, and low-voltage general service at L 6.5115, 24 cents.
ENEE's arrears to generators were twelve to thirteen months when the government took office and three to seven in September; the fuel supply to the thermal plants was a concern in September, and the dry season from December lowers hydro output in an El Niño year.
Fuel at the pump: diesel L 153.53 a gallon in Tegucigalpa since late September, 82% above January, held by a subsidy of L 5.05; regular petrol L 128.05 from 16 November after a peak of L 140.97; the fuel price index was 46.2% above a year earlier in October, the largest rise in the series; the state paid about USD 49 million in fuel support to September.
There is no gas network; factories run on grid power with diesel generators as back-up.
