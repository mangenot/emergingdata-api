[ed_overview date="16 November 2026"]
[ed_standfirst]Cambodia ends 2026 with its exports growing and its economy slowing. Garment, footwear and travel goods shipments rose 7.4% to USD 14.4 billion in ten months and total exports 20%, with the United States taking 44% of them at a 27% duty; growth has fallen to about 3%, reserves lost USD 5.3 billion between February and September, bad loans reached 9.6% of the book, and the fuel bill of the war in the Gulf is paid by a subsidy of more than USD 600 million. The Thai border has been closed for seventeen months, the scam economy brought a second round of US sanctions on 5 November, and the garment wage rises by two dollars to USD 212 in January.[/ed_standfirst]
<p>On 11 November the customs department published the trade figures for the first ten months: exports of USD 30.0 billion, up 20.4%, imports of USD 33.5 billion, up 21.2%, and garment, footwear and travel goods exports of USD 14.4 billion, up 7.4%, 48% of the total. The United States took 44.3% of exports and Vietnam 11.4%. The 10% duty added by the United States on 24 July does not show yet in the monthly series: apparel exports were USD 999 million in October, 1.4% above a year earlier, after a record USD 1.42 billion in July.</p>
<p>The same week brought the other side of the picture. On 5 November the prime minister said the government had spent more than USD 600 million since the spring to hold fuel and electricity prices, with petrol 31% and diesel 47% above their February level. The same day the US Treasury sanctioned four people and 41 companies linked to the Prince Group, the conglomerate whose founder was extradited to China in January, and on 9 November the government said that eighteen casino licences tied to the group had been revoked. On 31 October the Thai prime minister repeated that the border crossings, closed since June 2025, would stay shut until Thailand's territorial and security demands were met.</p>
<p>The Francophonie summit of 15 and 16 November gave the government a stage and its critics an audience: a UN expert asked for the release of more than a hundred detainees before the meeting, and the French president asked for an investigation into the murder of a former opposition deputy in Bangkok in January 2025. In Pursat and nine other provinces, floods since late September have displaced thousands and cut roads.</p>
<h3>The latest data</h3>
<p>The IMF concluded its Article IV consultation on 28 September with growth of 3.0% for 2026 after 5.3% in 2025, the weakest year outside the pandemic since the 1990s: higher energy costs, tourist arrivals down 44% to 2.25 million in January to August, remittances reduced by the return of nearly a million workers from Thailand, slower garment exports in the second half and a real estate correction in its third year. The World Bank puts the year at 3.5% and 2027 at 4%.</p>
<p>Inflation, 1.3% in January, reached 7.2% in May and was 5.3% in September, with food at 5.3%; the IMF expects 5.6% for the year and 2.9% in 2027. Reserves peaked at USD 29.2 billion in February and stood at USD 23.9 billion in September, after 25.3 billion in August, 9% below a year earlier and 7.2 months of imports; the National Bank reports them down 8.1% over the first half, and the IMF projects a current account deficit of about USD 4.7 billion, 8.7% of GDP, as fuel imports rise and tourism receipts fall.</p>
<p>Bank loans grew 4.6% to USD 64.2 billion in the year to June and non-performing loans rose to 9.6% of the book from 8.4%; the central bank ended the pandemic-era forbearance in September, so the ratio will rise further before it improves. The riel is at 4,073 to the dollar, within its band. The dollar is the currency of wages, prices and most credit, so the exchange rate carries none of the adjustment it carries in Bangladesh or Pakistan.</p>
<h3>The fuel bill</h3>
<p>Cambodia imports all of its petroleum and has no gas network, so the war in the Gulf arrived as a price, not as a shortage. Petrol rose from about 3,850 to 5,050 riel a litre between February and November and diesel from 3,850 to 5,650. The government cut import duties on fuel and pays Electricite du Cambodge to hold its tariffs, at a cost it put at USD 500 million on 7 October and more than USD 600 million on 5 November, to continue as long as world prices stay high.</p>
<p>The subsidy is what turns a budget deficit of 1% of GDP in 2025 into 2.9% in 2026, and what the IMF asks to phase out as prices ease, with targeted support in its place. For factories the effect is on trucking and generators rather than on the grid tariff, which has not moved; for workers it is in food prices, which the May peak of inflation reached through transport costs. The dry season from December brings the second exposure: hydropower falls and imports from Vietnam and Laos fill the gap, without the Thai supply that Cambodia stopped buying in June 2025.</p>
<h3>The Thai border</h3>
<p>The border has been closed to people and goods since June 2025, before the five days of fighting of 24 to 28 July that left more than forty dead and some 300,000 displaced on both sides. The Kuala Lumpur accord of 26 October 2025 broke down in early December; three weeks of fighting followed, and a second ceasefire has held since late December, with incidents in January and a Thai seizure of a Cambodian fishing boat in February. In May Thailand revoked the 2001 memorandum on the maritime boundary and Cambodia opened a conciliation under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, with sessions in Singapore in September and in The Hague in October; the military commissions met on 28 October.</p>
<p>For the supply chain the closure matters in three places. The factories of Poipet and Banteay Meanchey that relied on Thai inputs and Thai ports lost both, and three of them closed in August with about 4,000 workers. Nearly a million Cambodian workers came back from Thailand, which cut remittances and added labour supply to a garment sector of 1.1 million. And land tourism from Thailand, the largest source of arrivals, has gone.</p>
<h3>The scam economy</h3>
<p>The second external pressure is the scam industry, estimated by the US Institute of Peace at USD 12.5 billion a year, about a quarter of GDP. Chen Zhi, founder of the Prince Group, sanctioned by the United States and Britain in October 2025 and arrested on 6 January 2026, was extradited to China; the central bank ordered the liquidation of Prince Bank two days later; Taiwanese prosecutors indicted 62 people in March; the US Treasury added four people and 41 companies on 5 November; eighteen casino licences have been revoked and assets near USD 1 billion frozen.</p>
<p>The IMF put financial integrity among its main recommendations: examine the links between criminal proceeds and the banks, close licensing gaps, regulate virtual assets. For a buyer the exposure is indirect but real. A supplier's bank or shareholder can be named, correspondent banking can tighten, and the country's standing with the Financial Action Task Force, which took it off the grey list in 2023, is in question again.</p>
<h3>The financial position</h3>
<p>Against these pressures the state is lightly indebted and the external buffer is large. Public debt is 25% of GDP, almost all of it concessional, external debt 46% of GNI with debt service at 10% of exports, and the IMF rates the risk of debt distress low; Moody's holds B2 with a stable outlook since April. Reserves at USD 23.9 billion cover 7.2 months of imports, against four in Bangladesh and less than three in Pakistan. There is no IMF programme and none in prospect.</p>
<p>The weaknesses are inside the banking system rather than in the sovereign accounts: credit of about 120% of GDP built on real estate and microfinance, non-performing loans at 9.6% and rising as forbearance ends, credit growth down to 3.5%, deposits growing 4%, and a currency the central bank does not issue, which limits its support to the dollars it holds. The reserve fall of the first half, from 29.2 to 23.9 billion in September, has not been explained by the National Bank; the fuel bill, the loss of tourism and dollar liquidity for the banks are the candidates, and the September figure says that it does.</p>
<h3>The political settlement</h3>
<p>The Cambodian People's Party holds 120 of the 125 seats in the National Assembly. Hun Manet succeeded his father in August 2023; Hun Sen presides over the Senate and signs as head of state when the King is abroad. The opposition exists on conditions: Kem Sokha was pardoned on 25 May 2026 with a five-year ban from politics, the commune law was amended in September to give a party with two thirds of the vote all three council posts before the elections of June 2027, which also choose the Senate, press accreditation now requires a letter of good conduct, and two years of conscription apply to men aged 18 to 25 since the law of 23 May.</p>
<p>The result is a political risk that is low in frequency and high in concentration. GDELT counts 26 protest events in October, 0.7 times the yearly average and the lowest of the five countries covered, and there has been no strike wave since 2014; but wages, land, licences and taxes are decided by a small circle, and the sanctions of 2025 and 2026 reach that circle's business partners. The dispute with Thailand and the pressure on the scam economy are handled by the same people.</p>
<h3>Thirty years of one model</h3>
<p>The garment industry grew from the US quota agreement of 1999, which tied market access to labour standards, into 1,800 factories and 1.1 million workers, 48% of exports with footwear and travel goods, and the second highest legal wage of the five countries covered. The minimum wage is set in dollars each autumn by a tripartite council: USD 212 from January, 1% more than in 2026, against union demands of USD 225 to 250, and USD 232 to 243 with the attendance, transport and seniority allowances. The dollar wage removes the currency relief that Bangladesh or Pakistan obtain from a depreciation; it also removes the inflation surprise, since the scale is reset every year.</p>
<p>On the market side the United States takes 44% of all exports and about 40% of apparel, at a duty of 27% since July, with a tariff-rate quota promised and not established and a second Section 301 investigation, on excess capacity, due by March 2027 that does not list textiles. The EU takes about a fifth, duty-free under Everything But Arms except for the lines withdrawn in 2020, and the GSP regulation that applies from January 2027 keeps EBA for three years after the LDC graduation of December 2029, so to the end of 2032. Chinese, Korean and Japanese investment keeps adding capacity, and total exports grew three times faster than garments this year, a sign that the dependence on one product is easing.</p>
<h3>What it means for buyers</h3>
<p>The cost base for 2027 is known: USD 212 plus allowances from January, a grid tariff held by the budget, fuel at the world price, 27% into the United States and zero into the EU on most lines. The risks are of interruption and of standing rather than of cost. The Poipet cluster depends on a border that Bangkok keeps closed; the Water Festival of 23 to 25 November and the Khmer New Year of 14 to 16 April close factories for a week each; the dry season from December tests the grid; floods have cut roads in ten provinces since September.</p>
<p>Two items deserve a check at supplier level. The bank, because the sanctions lists and the liquidation of Prince Bank show that a Cambodian bank can disappear in a week. And the origin of inputs, because the 10% duty is a forced-labour measure and the excess-capacity case against China makes transshipment through Cambodia the next question US customs will ask.</p>

No public index quotes rates from Sihanoukville.

[ed_section key="ENE"]
[ed_lead]All fuel is imported and there is no gas network, so the war in the Gulf arrived as a price: petrol up 31% and diesel 47% since February, kept off the grid tariff by a subsidy of more than USD 600 million. Industrial power costs about 13.6 US cents per kWh, among the dearer of the five, and the dry season from December is when the hydro-based grid runs short.[/ed_lead]
[ed_text]
Electricite du Cambodge sells industrial power at about 13.6 US cents per kWh, a figure awaiting verification, dearer than Bangladesh and Pakistan and close to Kenya; the tariff has not moved in 2026 because the government pays the difference, together with cuts in fuel import duties, at a cost it put at USD 500 million on 7 October and more than USD 600 million on 5 November, to continue while world prices stay high.
Petrol was 5,050 riel a litre on 5 November, 31% above February, and diesel 5,650, 47% above; diesel is the cost that reaches factories through trucking and generators, and food prices through transport.
Supply is the second question: hydropower and coal, imports from Vietnam and Laos since Cambodia stopped buying Thai power in June 2025, utility-scale solar of about 1.5 GW by mid-2026 and a first grid battery; clean sources gave 62% of supply in 2025.
Dry-season shortfalls from December to May are met by imports and load management rather than by scheduled cuts in the industrial zones; grid losses are 10% of output.
The IMF asks for the subsidy to be phased out as prices ease, which would put the increase on the tariff.
[/ed_text]
