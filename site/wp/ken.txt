[ed_overview date="17 November 2026"]
[ed_standfirst]Kenya ends 2026 with the strongest external position in a decade and the weakest budget. Reserves of USD 14.6 billion and a shilling flat at 130 to the dollar for two years stand against a deficit heading for 7% of GDP, domestic interest absorbing a third of revenue and no IMF programme before the election of 10 August 2027. Inflation reached 7.3% in October, a forty-month high, on fuel a third dearer than a year ago, which sets up the Labour Day wage round of May. Apparel enters the United States duty-free again and AGOA runs to the end of 2028; the United States takes nine tenths of the garments, and shipments there were 3% higher over twelve months.[/ed_standfirst]
<p>On 14 November the energy regulator left pump prices unchanged for a second month, petrol at KES 226.96 a litre and diesel at 227.76 in Nairobi, by drawing KES 4.6 billion from the fuel levy fund; without it diesel would have risen another KES 10.54. The two decisions of the month are elsewhere: the Monetary Policy Committee of 8 December, with inflation at 7.3% in October against a ceiling of 7.5%, and the IMF mission expected in early December, the first Article IV since the programme lapsed in April 2025 and, as the governor said on 15 October, not the start of a new one.</p>
<p>The opposition held rallies in Kitengela on 8 November and in Kisii on 15 November to repeat that it will field one candidate against William Ruto on 10 August 2027, with Fred Matiang'i and Kalonzo Musyoka the names put forward and the former president Uhuru Kenyatta pressing for unity; the flag-bearer is not chosen. Nine months out, the election is the political date of the year ahead and the budget of June 2027 the last fiscal one before it.</p>
<p>The reserves stood at USD 14.6 billion on 12 November, 5.9 months of imports, the shilling at 129.96 to the dollar, where it has been since mid-2024, and the overnight rate at 8.75%. Remittances were USD 430 million in October, 2% below a year earlier, and USD 5.0 billion over twelve months.</p>
<h3>The latest data</h3>
<p>Inflation rose from 4.3% in February to 7.3% in October, the highest in forty months, with transport at 17.1% and food at 10.2%: the fuel prices of the war in the Gulf, passed through in October and held in November, and food after a poor long-rains season. Core inflation is 4.3%. The central bank held its rate at 8.75% on 7 October, the level since February, and expects inflation to stay inside the 2.5 to 7.5% band; the December meeting decides whether the cycle that brought the rate down from 13% is over.</p>
<p>Growth is the strong part: 5.0% expected for 2026 after 4.6%, and 5.3% in 2027 (central bank); S&P puts 2026 at 4.9%. Private credit grows 10.6% a year, lending rates average 14.4%, and non-performing loans have come down from 17.6% in August 2025 to 13.9% in September, still the highest ratio of the five countries covered. Goods exports rose 11.8% in the year to August on tea, horticulture and transport equipment, imports 15.8%, the current account deficit widened to 3.1% of GDP, and tourism receipts rose 8.7%.</p>
<p>The budget is the weak part. Public debt was KES 13.0 trillion at the end of June, 68.5% of GDP on the Treasury's measure and 71% on the IMF's, 44% of it external; domestic interest alone cost KES 863 billion in 2025/26, about a third of ordinary revenue. S&P, which affirmed B with a stable outlook in August, and Moody's both expect a deficit of 7.1% of GDP in 2026/27 against the Treasury's 5.5%, on revenue shortfalls and pre-election spending.</p>
<h3>The fuel bill</h3>
<p>Kenya imports all of its refined fuel and buys its electricity in dollars. Diesel at KES 227.76 is 33% dearer than a year ago and petrol 23%; VAT on fuel has been cut to 8% and the levy fund pays the rest, KES 4.6 billion for the November cycle. On electricity the fuel and foreign exchange charges on bills move every month (KES 3.14 and 0.72 per kWh in June) because the power purchase agreements are paid in dollars; the industrial tariff itself has not changed since the 2023 order, Kenya Power having withdrawn its application for a 2026 to 2029 review in June.</p>
<p>For a factory the hit is on transport and on the cost of living of its workers more than on the grid, which is about nine tenths geothermal, hydro, wind and solar. The second-round effect is the wage: the minimum wage rose 12% on 1 May 2026 after 6% in 2024 and 12% in 2022, following inflation with a lag, and 7% inflation in an election year makes a rise in May 2027 likely.</p>
<h3>The election</h3>
<p>The vote of 10 August 2027 is the first since the protests of June 2024, when the Finance Bill was withdrawn after parliament was stormed, and those of June and July 2025, which left more than fifty dead. The anniversary of 25 June 2026 passed under a police lockdown with hundreds of arrests and no deaths; GDELT counted 406 protest events that month, twice the average, and 183 in October, in line with it. Raila Odinga died in October 2025; his party sits in the government since the 2024 crisis and part of its leadership in the opposition that is forming, with Gachagua, Kalonzo Musyoka, Matiang'i, Karua and Nyoro, which has agreed on one candidate and not on the name.</p>
<p>What an election does to the economy is known from 2017 and 2022: spending rises in the June budget, the shilling and the bond market wait, and investment decisions are deferred for two quarters. Kenya's elections have gone to court twice, annulled in 2017 and upheld in 2022, and to violence once in this generation, in 2007. The rating agencies have written the election into their outlooks and the IMF into the timing of any programme.</p>
<h3>The IMF question</h3>
<p>The 2021 programme of USD 3.6 billion ended in April 2025 without its ninth review; a successor was requested in March 2025, and four missions, in September and October 2025 and February and March 2026, did not produce an agreement. The governor said on 15 October that the next programme has no start date because the two sides have not agreed on its shape; the December mission is an Article IV. Meanwhile the Treasury finances itself in the market: a USD 2.25 billion Eurobond in February 2026, at 7.875% to 2034 and 8.7% to 2039, of which USD 500 million bought back 2028 and 2032 notes, a further issue under study, a World Bank development policy loan of about KES 94 billion and an African Development Bank loan of KES 34 billion, both tied to reforms. The Eurobond of May 2027, largely retired in February 2025, is no longer the cliff it was.</p>
<p>The cost is in the coupon and in the domestic market: 364-day bills at 9.0% with inflation at 7.3%, lending rates at 14.4%, domestic debt 56% of the total. The reserves were built from Eurobonds, remittances and the IMF's last disbursements, which is why they can fall as fast as they rose; the reference is 2023, when the shilling went from 123 to 160 before the buyback of early 2024 turned it round.</p>
<h3>Market access</h3>
<p>Kenyan apparel enters the United States duty-free again since 24 July 2026, when the 10% Section 122 surcharge expired; Kenya is not covered by the Section 301 duties of July, and the AGOA extension signed on 4 September runs to 31 December 2028 with the third-country fabric rule intact and refunds of the duties paid during the lapse of October 2025 to February 2026. US imports of apparel from Kenya were USD 625 million over the twelve months to August, 3% more than a year earlier, and USD 68 million in August alone, 22% above August 2025, the catch-up after the tariff year. The EU and the United Kingdom are duty-free under the economic partnership agreements, and China's zero tariff on 98% of lines applies since May.</p>
<p>Two things qualify the access. The annual AGOA eligibility review, which looks at governance and human rights, was reported in July to be scrutinising Kenya, and the United States opened talks on a reciprocal trade framework in February; AGOA is a unilateral preference that a proclamation can withdraw, and the 2028 expiry needs a new act in an American election year.</p>
<h3>Twenty-five years of AGOA</h3>
<p>The export zones were built on AGOA from 2001: 43 apparel firms, more than 66,000 direct jobs, about USD 600 million of exports to the United States, which takes nine tenths of the garments, and 70% of all Kenyan exports to that market. The model is cut-make-trim on imported fabric, which the third-country fabric provision allows and which keeps value added low. The wage is the second lowest of the five countries covered, USD 139 a month (KES 18,047 for a general labourer in the cities, plus a 15% housing allowance), the electricity among the dearest, the port one of the better connected on the East African coast. Job creation in the zones fell 44% in the year to June 2026, to 6,732, on the AGOA uncertainty and delayed projects.</p>
<p>The cost base moves with Nairobi, not with the dollar: a flat shilling and 7% inflation raise dollar costs by 3 to 4% a year, and the wage order follows inflation on Labour Day. The social charges have risen faster than the wage: the housing levy of 1.5% since 2024, the health insurance contribution of 2.75% since October 2024, and the pension tiers that rise each February until 2027.</p>
<h3>What it means for buyers</h3>
<p>The risk calendar for 2027 is fiscal and political: the Labour Day wage announcement, the June budget and Finance Bill (the 2024 one brought the country to a halt) and the vote of 10 August with its two quarters of deferred decisions. Costs are stable in shillings and rising in dollars; power is reliable by regional standards and dear; the Mombasa corridor and the long rains of March to May are the physical risk, and a severe El Nino is on the central bank's list for 2027.</p>
<p>Three checks at supplier level: AGOA eligibility and the origin paperwork behind the duty refunds; the pass-through charges on power bills, which move every month; and the terminal dues disputes that follow factory closures in the zones, which the EPZ authority has had to mediate this year.</p>

[ed_section key="ENE"]
[ed_lead]About 13.5 US cents per kWh for medium industry, pending verification, among the dearer of the five countries covered, on a grid that is nine tenths renewable and loses a quarter of its output; no industrial gas. Diesel is a third dearer than a year ago and held only by the levy fund; the tariff review has been withdrawn, so the grid price stays until a new order.[/ed_lead]
[ed_text]
The medium-industry tariff (CI2, 11 kV) combines the energy charge of the 2023 tariff order with monthly pass-through charges for fuel and foreign exchange and 16% VAT, about KES 17.5 per kWh before the demand charge, or 13.5 US cents, a figure awaiting verification; Kenya Power filed for a 2026 to 2029 review on 31 March and withdrew it in June, so the 2023 order stays in force until a new one is approved.
The pass-through charges move every month, KES 3.14 for fuel and 0.72 for foreign exchange per kWh in June, because the power purchase agreements are paid in dollars.
The grid is geothermal, hydro, wind and solar for about nine tenths, which limits the fuel pass-through, but transmission and distribution losses of 26% of output are the highest of the five after Honduras.
There is no gas network; factories use grid power, heavy fuel oil, LPG and biomass.
Fuel is where the war in the Gulf arrived: diesel at KES 227.76 a litre in Nairobi from 15 November, 33% above a year earlier, and petrol at 226.96, 23% above, both held for two months by KES 4.6 billion from the levy fund and VAT at 8%; the fund is finite and the next review falls on 14 December.
[/ed_text]
