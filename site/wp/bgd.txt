[ed_overview date="12 November 2026"]
[ed_standfirst]Bangladesh reaches the end of 2026 with its labour cost, its political order and its energy bill in question at the same time. The external accounts are sounder than two years ago, with reserves near USD 30 billion and remittances at record levels, but the war in the Gulf has doubled the cost of fuel imports, the garment minimum wage is under revision with a demand of Tk 30,000 against the current 12,500, and a series of attacks precedes the announced return of the former prime minister.[/ed_standfirst]
<p>Between 8 and 10 November more than a hundred crude bombs exploded in Dhaka, Manikganj and other districts; buses were set on fire and a driver was killed. The police attribute the attacks to paid recruits of a banned organisation; the government attributes them to supporters of Sheikh Hasina, who was sentenced to death in absentia in 2025 and has announced her return from India in December.</p>
<p>In the same two weeks the garment industry was affected by strikes. Protests that began on 31 October in Mirpur, Savar, Ashulia and Gazipur led to the temporary closure of about 500 factories, and on 12 November about 1,500 workers from six to eight factories blocked the Dhaka-Tangail highway at Kaliakair before being dispersed. The demand is a minimum wage of Tk 30,000 a month; the current scale, set in December 2023, is Tk 12,500, about USD 100.</p>
<p>The government formed the Minimum Wage Board on 1 November. The workers' representatives have proposed Tk 32,000, the employers have four working days to reply, and the government intends to apply the new scale from December with payment in January. The manufacturers' association has answered with eleven proposals, beginning with a "no work, no pay" rule.</p>
<h3>The latest data</h3>
<p>The data published in early November describe an economy that is growing but under pressure from costs. Exports rose 4.4% in October to USD 3.99 billion and garment exports 4.9% to USD 3.17 billion, the third consecutive month of growth after a flat year; garment exports for July to October reached USD 13.75 billion, 5.8% more than a year earlier. The knitwear association considers the result negative in real terms, since raw material costs rose 15 to 20% and wages 9%.</p>
<p>Remittances amounted to USD 2.87 billion in October, 12% more than a year earlier but below USD 3 billion for the fifth consecutive month; the central bank links the slowdown to the higher cost of living of migrant workers in the Gulf. Inflation eased to 8.27% from 8.34%, with food inflation falling to 6.4% and non-food inflation rising to 9.9%.</p>
<p>Reserves stood at USD 29.98 billion on the BPM6 definition (USD 34.85 billion gross) on 6 November, after a payment of USD 1.64 billion to the Asian Clearing Union, equivalent to about four months of imports. The taka traded at 124.3 to the dollar, after a year between 122 and 123.</p>
<h3>The energy bill</h3>
<p>The war in the Gulf explains the rise in non-food inflation and in the import bill. Bangladesh imports almost half of its energy from the Middle East. Payments for petroleum imports doubled to USD 2.49 billion in July and August; spot LNG prices have almost tripled since the beginning of the conflict; LNG deliveries fell by 83% between July and August after the disruption of traffic through the Strait of Hormuz and a fire at one of the country's two floating regasification terminals.</p>
<p>The electricity deficit exceeds 3,000 MW, factories depend on diesel generators at a fuel price 70% higher than before the war, and one of the largest groups estimates its daily energy expenditure at USD 300,000 against USD 200,000 previously. More than 400 small factories closed in the three years to June 2026. The wage claim therefore reaches an industry whose margins have already been reduced by energy costs.</p>
<h3>The financial position</h3>
<p>The financial position is stronger than in 2024 but has weaknesses that the reserves figure does not show. Reserves had fallen below USD 19 billion at the end of 2024 and were rebuilt by remittances, which reached a record USD 35.5 billion in fiscal year 2026, and by exports; the import restrictions introduced in 2022 have been relaxed but remain in place. Public debt is moderate, at 42% of GDP. The banking system is the main weakness: non-performing loans represented 31% of the portfolio at the end of 2025 after a peak of 36%, the policy rate is 9.5% and private credit grows by 4.6% a year.</p>
<p>The government set aside the USD 5.5 billion IMF programme negotiated by the interim administration and is negotiating a new USD 4.5 billion facility over three years; a mission is expected in November and an agreement is possible in December. The conditions under discussion include a market-determined exchange rate, a reduction of tax exemptions, lower subsidies, including on energy, and a bankruptcy law. In the short term each of these measures raises costs for the industry; their purpose is to lower the country's financial risk over the medium term.</p>
<h3>The political settlement</h3>
<p>The political framework is recent. The uprising of July and August 2024 ended fifteen years of Awami League government; the interim government banned the party's activities in May 2025; the election of 12 February 2026 gave the BNP a majority and brought Tarique Rahman to the premiership, without the participation of the party that had dominated the previous decade.</p>
<p>This is the context of the November attacks: a large political organisation without legal representation and a leader who intends to return. The government has requested her extradition and reinforced security. The count of protest events compiled from GDELT, 200 to 350 a month since March compared with 600 to 1,000 before the election, indicates a calm period that November is interrupting.</p>
<h3>Forty years of one model</h3>
<p>Over four decades Bangladesh built the second largest garment export industry in the world on the basis of low production costs. Garments brought USD 38.7 billion in the last fiscal year, more than 80% of export earnings, and employ about four million people. The model delivered growth and foreign exchange, and the exit from least developed country status, which the United Nations postponed on 29 October, at Bangladesh's request, from 24 November 2026 to 24 November 2029; duty-free access to the European Union under Everything But Arms therefore continues until at least 2032.</p>
<p>The model also kept wages among the lowest in the world, about USD 100 a month against USD 139 in Kenya and USD 144 in Pakistan, and produced a distribution of income that has been contested in 2023, in 2024 and again now. The wage revision of 2026 is therefore more than a periodic adjustment. It tests whether the new government can raise the price of labour without losing the orders on which the external balance depends.</p>
<h3>What it means for buyers</h3>
<p>The outlook is a rising cost base from January and a risk of interruptions rather than of a breakdown. The wage award will lie between the employers' counter-proposal and the Tk 30,000 demanded; the 26% duty in the United States has applied since July; energy surcharges and a weaker taka add to it.</p>
<p>The interruptions to anticipate are factory closures during strikes, road blockades on the Dhaka-Tangail and Dhaka-Chattogram routes, power rationing during the dry season, and a month of December in which the former prime minister's return, the wage gazette and the IMF agreement coincide. Access to the EU market remains duty-free, reserves cover four months of imports, and the condition of the banks remains the point to verify for each supplier.</p>

[ed_section key="ENE"]
[ed_lead]Grid power is the cheapest of the five countries covered at about 10.3 US cents per kWh, but the supply is the problem: LNG deliveries fell 83% between July and August, the shortfall exceeds 3,000 MW, and factories run on diesel that costs 70% more than before the war.[/ed_lead]
[ed_text]
The industrial electricity tariff rose 16.7% in June 2026 to Tk 12.52 to 16.36 per kWh, about 10.3 US cents, still the lowest of the five; gas for new industrial connections was raised 33% in April 2025 to Tk 40 to 42 per cubic metre, about USD 7 per MMBtu.
The war changed the supply rather than the tariff: spot LNG has nearly tripled, deliveries fell 83% between July and August after the Hormuz disruption and a fire at one of the two regasification terminals, the shortfall exceeds 3,000 MW, and diesel for factory generators costs 70% more; one large group's daily energy bill went from USD 200,000 to 300,000, and more than 400 small factories closed in three years.
The answers are cheaper solar imports, about 200 MW added in the first half, and the Rooppur nuclear plant expected in December.
A tariff that does not follow LNG is paid by the budget, which is what the IMF conditions of section 1 address.
[/ed_text]
